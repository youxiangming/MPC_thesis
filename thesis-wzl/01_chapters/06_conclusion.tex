\chapter{Conclusion and Outlook}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This chapter closes the thesis by returning to the objective of
\cref{sec:objectives}: integrate whole-body \mpc{} on the physical
\hero{} platform and characterise its \ee{}-tracking behaviour. The
preceding chapters already separate what the optimiser does on its
kinematic model (\cref{ch:simulation}), what the MRT bridge adds
(\cref{sec:hw-challenges}), and what the plant adds on top
(\cref{sec:hw-results}). What follows is a concise account of what was
achieved, what the present data do and do not establish, and which
engineering steps would most directly change the numbers reported in
\cref{ch:hardware}.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:concl-summary}

The work set out to move \ocs{} whole-body \mpc{} from a framework
demonstration to a deployable stack on a differential-drive mobile
manipulator in an LMAS-oriented setting. Against that goal, four
contributions were delivered.

First, a whole-body motion-planning approach was selected for the
\hero{} platform after comparing \ocs{} with alternative frameworks
(\cref{ch:sota}). Second, \ocs{} was integrated into the existing
software stack: a deployment over Zenoh keeps the actuation path local
to the IPC while the SLQ solver runs on an
external PC (\cref{sec:distributed-arch}); a nine-state kinematic model
with non-holonomic base, safety-panel joint limits, and a reduced
self-collision set was parameterised for on-robot operation (\cref{sec:wholebody-model});
and an MRT bridge turns the published policy into wheel and joint
commands under explicit guards (\cref{sec:hw-interfaces}). Third, a
simulation-based acceptance path was built around Experiment~A: the
task file and waypoint list that the robot loads are exercised in a
dummy MRT loop, internal metrology and ISO-oriented path metrics score
that loop and the hardware laps alike, and on the archived files the
dummy loop tracks the commanded box at \qty{11}{\milli\meter} RMS
(\cref{tab:sim-residual}). Fourth, that
configuration was taken through mock \texttt{ros2\_control} hardware and
onto the physical platform. The closed eight-corner path of
\cref{fig:sim-box} completed on both plants; the approaching gate,
segment tagging, and lead-out hold that \cref{sec:sim-node} specifies all
appeared in the recorded sessions.

The result these contributions produce is a gap, and the gap is the
outcome this work reports. The same Experiment~A files were executed on
three plants that differ only in what lies below the policy. The dummy
loop, where nothing but the optimiser acts, tracks the commanded box at
\qty{11}{\milli\meter} RMS (\cref{tab:sim-residual}). Mock hardware,
which adds the bridge, its clamps and a loop bound to the wall clock,
tracks at \qty{27}{\milli\meter}. The physical platform, with the same
files and the same lap duration, tracks at \qty{130}{\milli\meter} and
does not recover during the corner dwells
(\cref{fig:hw-error,tab:hw-metrics,tab:hw-segments}). Geometry, the
\ocs{} time axis and the cost tuning are common to all three and are
therefore not what separates them.

What separates them is where the controller's correction sits inside the
plan. The loop ran in real time in the sense of \cref{eq:rt-invariant},
with no policy-exhaustion hold on the hardware lap, but at an effective
\qty{4.4}{\hz} set by the solve time, so the bridge reads each policy a
mean \qty{0.361}{\second} after the state it was computed for
(\cref{fig:hw-lag}). For the ten policies of that lap whose full input
trajectory was recorded, the base command does not merely decay over
that interval: it opens near the \qty{0.1}{\meter\per\second} bound,
crosses zero at a median $\tau = \qty{0.235}{\second}$, and has reversed
sign by the time the bridge samples it (\cref{fig:hw-policy-u}). Over
that sample the robot receives a base command opposite to the one the
solver computed. The mock lap, read the same way, carries no such pulse
at all: its base command starts an order of magnitude below the bound
and barely changes across its own lag. Both plants read their policies
late. Only the physical platform provokes a plan that is unsafe to read
late.

Several constraints bound how strongly these results can be read. All
desired and attained poses were reconstructed from wheel odometry and
joint encoders inside the waypoint node (\cref{sec:sim-metrology}), so
the scores measure internal consistency of the closed loop, not absolute
accuracy in the hall. Each plant was exercised once, at
$v_{\mathrm{ref}}=\qty{0.05}{\meter\per\second}$, without the three
mandatory ISO speed levels or repeated cycles~\cite{ISO9283}. The
PC was connected by Ethernet throughout
(\cref{sec:hw-deployment}); the numbers therefore do not speak to
control over the platform's wireless link. The \ocs{} model treats the
base as a velocity integrator and knows nothing of the drive
controller's acceleration limit or of the UR10e following error under
streaming position
control~\cite{HenkelRoth2019freeMoVe,UR10e}.

\cref{tab:requirements}, what realises each one in the integrated system,
and the evidence reported above for it.

\section{Critical Reflection}
\label{sec:concl-reflection}

\begin{sloppypar}
The experiments answer a narrower question than the LMAS motivation of
\cref{sec:lmas} might suggest. They show that a time-parameterised
\ee{} reference, published as \ocs{} \texttt{TargetTrajectories} rather
than as a sequence of static goals, can be executed end to end on
\hero{} hardware by a receding-horizon whole-body controller, and they
localise the largest tracking gap to the plant and to the policy lag
acting on a model that treats that plant as an integrator, rather than
to a broken data pipeline or to a reference that the optimiser cannot
track on its own model. They do not, by themselves, establish that this
pipeline meets production accuracy requirements, nor that whole-body
\mpc{} outperforms a well-tuned decoupled stack on task completion time
or corner accuracy.
\end{sloppypar}

The metric layer deserves the same caution. Time-aligned pose error
$e_{\mathrm{p}}$ and the along-track lag $t_{\mathrm{lag}}$ of
\cref{eq:lag-eff} are meaningful comparisons \emph{within} one
controller, because desired and attained poses share the same
observation time and the same state estimate. Hausdorff distance and its
mean and percentile variants (\cref{eq:mean-hausdorff}) remove the
assumption of a common speed profile, but with $n=1$ cycle they still
do not yield ISO path accuracy $\mathrm{AT}_{p}$ or repeatability
$\mathrm{RT}_{p}$ in the sense of ISO~9283~\cite{ISO9283}.

The split between mock and hardware results is instructive in what it
rules out. Mock hardware preserves policy lag, velocity clamps, and the
Zenoh hop; since it tracks at \qty{27}{\milli\meter} RMS, the reference
geometry, the \ocs{} time axis, and the cost tuning are not the dominant
fault. What remains has two
parts, and they carry different weight. The sign reversal of
\cref{fig:hw-policy-u} is measured, and it explains why an error that
exceeds the pure lag prediction $0.36\,\mathrm{s}\times
v_{\mathrm{ref}}$ can persist through a dwell: a command of the wrong
sign does not stop being wrong when the reference stops moving. Why the
solver places its correction there is the second part, and it is still
inferred rather than shown. The kinematic integrator of
\cref{sec:wholebody-model} lets the base velocity step to its
\qty{0.1}{\meter\per\second} bound at $\tau=0$, and nothing in the model
charges the \qty{400}{\kilo\gram} platform for the acceleration that
step would require, so a plan that front-loads the correction is optimal
on the model and infeasible on the robot.
Separating that from wheel slip, odometry drift and arm tracking error
would need additional sensing or deliberately simpler motions.

Scope limits are finally worth stating plainly. One mobile manipulator,
one closed path, and one lap per plant are enough to debug integration
and to expose the main failure modes; they are not enough to support
statistical claims about repeatability or about behaviour across the
workspace. The waypoint node of \cref{sec:sim-node} extends the upstream
\ocs{} example, which regulates to a single goal set by an interactive
marker in RViz, to the tracking of a time-parameterised
trajectory~\cite{ocs2Toolbox}. The evaluation has so far exercised only
that tracking regime, on a single box geometry.

\section{Recommendations for Resolving Present Issues}
\label{sec:concl-recommendations}

The hardware lap already identifies where effort would change the
recorded numbers, rather than merely widen the solution time window.

\paragraph{Raise the effective \mpc{} rate and shorten policy lag.}
Mean solve time on the hardware session was \qty{227}{\milli\second}
(\cref{tab:hw-metrics}), so the controller replans at roughly
\qty{4.4}{\hz}, a rate set by the solve time rather than by the task
file (\cref{sec:hw-interfaces}). The DDP benchmark that \ocs{} prints at
shutdown attributes that time to two stages above all. In an earlier
instrumented session on the same node, summarised over $1794$
iterations, \emph{Initialization} averaged \qty{170}{\milli\second} per
iteration (\qty{56}{\percent} of solver runtime) and \emph{Search
Strategy} \qty{111}{\milli\second} (\qty{36}{\percent}), while the
backward pass stayed below \qty{1}{\milli\second}. The absolute values
belong to that session rather than to the laps of \cref{ch:hardware},
but the split suggests attacking warm-start cost and rollout search
before adding SLQ iterations. Pinning the solver threads
away from unrelated PC load, keeping the Release build used here,
and revisiting the twelve-pair self-collision set if profiling shows
that distance queries dominate those two stages are practical first
steps. Enabling a feedback policy
(\texttt{useFeedbackPolicy=true} in the task file; currently disabled in
\cref{tab:mpc-config}) would let the bridge use linear gains between
sparse policy updates~\cite{Grandia2019FeedbackMPC,ocs2Toolbox}; the
trade-off is larger policy messages over Zenoh, which must be measured on
the link that will actually be used in production. Widening $T_{\mathrm{sol}}$ further, as in the Experiment~A
file, prevents exhaustion when a solve spikes; it does not restore the
aggressive $\tau=0$ input that lag already consumes (\cref{fig:hw-lag}).

\paragraph{Close the model--plant gap on the base.}
The two largest per-edge residuals on the robot are on horizontal edges
(edges 3 and~6 of \cref{tab:hw-segments}), where the base can take part
in the motion, and the residual does not relax when the reference stops.
Adding an acceleration limit or a first-order lag on base velocities
inside the \ocs{} model would align the optimiser with what the
freeMoVe drive stack enforces and reduce the state mismatch that each
re-solve inherits~\cite{HenkelRoth2019freeMoVe}. A complementary
experiment---base motion on a closed planar path with the \ee{} held
fixed in space, the converse of Experiment~A---would separate base
tracking from arm-dominated edges without changing the software
architecture.

\paragraph{Improve pose estimation.}
\begin{sloppypar}
If odometry drift dominates closed-loop drift, fusing wheel odometry
with additional proprioceptive or exteroceptive sources through an
extended Kalman filter (for example the \texttt{robot\_localization}
package) is a direct next step; the present pipeline uses raw wheel
odometry only
(\cref{sec:hero-platform})~\cite{Moore2015RobotLocalization}.
\end{sloppypar}

\paragraph{Complete the evaluation programme.}
Two items would complete the present characterisation. ISO
Table~3 speed levels and $n\ge 3$ cycles on the whole-body pipeline would
populate $\mathrm{AT}_{p}$ and $\mathrm{RT}_{p}$~\cite{ISO9283}.
Re-running the mock versus hardware comparison over a wireless Zenoh link
would test whether the Ethernet sessions of \cref{sec:hw-deployment} bias
the timing budget optimistically~\cite{zenoh}.

\paragraph{Run the lag-compensation experiment first.}
One experiment is prior to every item above. The sign reversal of
\cref{fig:hw-policy-u} predicts that the hardware residual is driven by
\emph{when} the policy is read rather than by what the plant does with
it, and that prediction is falsifiable on the same robot with the same
files: re-run the lap with the lag compensated, and measure whether the
\qty{130}{\milli\meter} moves towards the \qty{27}{\milli\meter} of the
mock plant. The lag can be compensated by the feedback policy named
above, by starting each solve from the state predicted for the instant
at which its policy will be read, or by a solve short enough that the
bridge reads the prefix it now skips. If the residual moves, the gap is
a timing problem and the model can stay kinematic. If it does not, the
correction sits in the model, and an acceleration-limited base is the
next thing to build. The recorded laps cannot decide between these two
readings (\cref{sec:concl-reflection}); this measurement can.
\Cref{fig:deciding-experiment} lays out that decision.

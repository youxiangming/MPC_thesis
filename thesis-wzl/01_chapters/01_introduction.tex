\section{Line-less Mobile Assembly Systems and Their Implications for
  Motion Planning}
\label{sec:lmas}

Manufacturing is increasingly shaped by short product life cycles, high
variant diversity, and volatile demand. Rigid, station-based assembly
lines are optimised for a fixed product mix and a stable takt time. When
the product or the required volume changes, the layout, material flow,
and station balancing must be reconfigured, which is slow and
capital-intensive~\cite{Huettemann2016LMAS}. The flexibility that
volatile demand requires and the rigidity of a fixed line pull in
opposite directions, which has motivated alternative production
paradigms.

The \emph{Line-less Mobile Assembly System} (LMAS) is one such paradigm
studied at the Laboratory for Machine Tools and Production Engineering
(WZL) of RWTH Aachen University~\cite{Kern2019LMAS,Buckhorst2022LMAS}.
Instead of fixing products, tools, and workers to a sequence of static
stations, an LMAS treats all production resources---mobile robots,
mobile manipulators, workpiece carriers, and human workers---as
\emph{mobile, freely relocatable entities} in a shared shop floor. The
assembly ``line'' is dissolved into a dynamically reconfigurable
constellation of resources that are matched to the current order at run
time. This yields a spatially and temporally flexible production system
that can absorb product changes, demand fluctuations, and disturbances
without a physical relayout.

A central enabler of the LMAS concept is the \emph{mobile manipulator}: a
robotic arm mounted on a mobile base. The mobile base provides an
effectively unbounded workspace and the ability to reposition itself
between and within tasks, while the arm provides the dexterity required
for the assembly operation itself. Combining the two allows a single
resource to service many workstations and to reach parts of a large
workpiece that no fixed-base arm could cover.

This freedom changes the motion-planning problem. In a classical line, an industrial arm operates in a
carefully engineered, static cell: the part arrives at a known pose, the
surroundings are fixed, and the arm executes largely pre-programmed
trajectories. In an LMAS, several assumptions no longer hold:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Large, base-dependent workspace.} The target \ee{} pose
        may lie outside the reach of the arm from the current base pose,
        so the base and the arm must \emph{cooperate} to accomplish a
        single task.
  \item \textbf{Changing, shared environment.} Resources move relative to
        one another; the environment is neither static nor fully known in
        advance, so trajectories cannot be fully pre-computed offline.
  \item \textbf{Kinematic redundancy.} A mobile base combined with a
        six-degree-of-freedom (\SI{6}{DOF}) arm is highly redundant with
        respect to an \ee{} task. This redundancy is an opportunity---it
        can be exploited for reach, manipulability, or obstacle
        avoidance---but only if the planner reasons about the base and
        the arm \emph{jointly}.
\end{itemize}

Together, these conditions call for motion planning that is
\emph{reactive}, that spans the \emph{combined} configuration space of
base and arm, and that resolves the resulting redundancy deliberately
rather than as a by-product. Whole-body motion planning, introduced
next, is aimed at exactly this problem.

\section{The Need for Whole-Body Motion Planning: From Sequential to
  Unified Approaches}
\label{sec:need-wholebody}

The most common way to command a mobile manipulator is to treat the base
and the arm as two independent subsystems and to plan for them
\emph{sequentially}: a navigation stack first drives the base to a
suitable stand-off pose, and only then does an arm-motion planner (for
example, a sampling-based planner behind MoveIt~\cite{moveit2}) compute a
trajectory for the arm from that fixed base pose. This decoupled scheme
is simple, modular, and reuses mature, well-tested components, which
explains its prevalence in industry.

The decoupling, however, has intrinsic limitations that become severe in
the LMAS setting:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Loss of optimality and reach.} Choosing the base pose
        \emph{before} planning the arm discards the coupling between the
        two subsystems. A base pose that looks reasonable geometrically
        may leave the arm near a singularity or a joint limit, or may
        make the \ee{} target unreachable, even though a jointly chosen
        base--arm configuration exists.
  \item \textbf{Stop-and-go behaviour.} Sequential execution---drive,
        stop, then manipulate---prevents the base and the arm from moving
        \emph{simultaneously}. Tasks in which the \ee{} must follow a
        continuous path longer than the arm's reach (a common LMAS case)
        cannot be executed smoothly this way.
  \item \textbf{Poor disturbance rejection.} A plan computed once, for a
        fixed base pose, cannot react to base drift, moving obstacles, or
        an updated target without re-triggering the whole pipeline.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Whole-body motion planning} removes this artificial boundary by
treating the mobile base and the arm as a \emph{single} redundant
kinematic (or dynamic) system and planning their motion in one unified
optimisation. The \ee{} task is expressed directly in task space, and the
planner is free to distribute the required motion between the base and the
arm according to a chosen cost criterion. This directly
addresses the limitations above: the full combined workspace becomes
available, base and arm move simultaneously along one coordinated
trajectory, and, once embedded in a feedback loop, the plan reacts
continuously to a changing target and environment.

Formulating whole-body planning as a receding-horizon optimal-control
problem, i.e.\ as \emph{Model Predictive Control}
(\mpc{})~\cite{Rawlings2017MPC}, is well suited to mobile
manipulation. At each control step the planner solves a finite-horizon
optimisation over the whole-body state and input, applies the first part
of the resulting policy, and re-plans as new state estimates arrive.
Constraints such as joint and velocity limits, self-collision, and the
non-holonomic behaviour of a differential-drive base are handled inside
the same optimisation, and the receding-horizon re-planning provides the
reactivity that the LMAS setting requires. The open-source
\ocs{}~(Optimal Control for Switched Systems)
framework~\cite{ocs2Toolbox} provides a mature implementation of this
approach and has been demonstrated for whole-body loco-manipulation on
legged and wheeled platforms~\cite{Sleiman2021UnifiedMPC}. It is the
framework integrated and evaluated in this work.

\section{Objectives and Structure of This Work}
\label{sec:objectives}

\paragraph{Objective.}
The goal of this work is to \emph{integrate and validate} whole-body
\mpc{}, based on the \ocs{} framework, on the \hero{} mobile
manipulator---a differential-drive mobile base carrying a Universal
Robots UR10e arm~\cite{UR10e}---and to characterise the resulting
\ee{}-pose tracking behaviour under realistic conditions. In contrast to
studies that remain in simulation, the emphasis here is on a
\emph{deployable} system: the \mpc{} runs in a distributed,
real-time software architecture on the physical robot, and its
performance is assessed on hardware.

\paragraph{Scope and contributions.}
The concrete contributions of this thesis are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the selection of a suitable whole-body motion-planning approach
        for the \hero{} platform, following a comparison of candidate
        frameworks (\cref{ch:sota});
  \item the integration of \ocs{} whole-body \mpc{} into the \hero{}
        software stack, including a distributed host/on-board
        architecture for real-time operation, the whole-body model and
        its parameterisation, and the hardware and \mbox{ROS-control}
        interfaces (\cref{ch:integration});
  \item a simulation-based validation of the closed loop that isolates
        the geometry, timing, and data pipeline before hardware
        deployment (\cref{ch:simulation}); and
  \item the deployment of the system on the physical \hero{} platform,
        together with a discussion of the deployment challenges
        encountered and preliminary \ee{}-tracking results
        (\cref{ch:hardware}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Structure.}
The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows.
\Cref{ch:sota} reviews the state of the art in whole-body motion planning
for mobile manipulators, classifies the main approaches, presents
\ocs{} and alternative frameworks, and justifies the selection made here.
\Cref{ch:integration} describes the \hero{} platform and the integration
of \ocs{} into it: the distributed architecture, the whole-body model and
its parameterisation, and the interfaces to the low-level control layer.
\Cref{ch:simulation} presents the simulation-based validation using a
kinematic closed-loop (dummy MRT) setup. \Cref{ch:hardware} reports on
the hardware deployment, the challenges and their mitigations, and
preliminary \ee{}-tracking results. \Cref{ch:conclusion} summarises the
work, reflects critically on its limitations, and gives recommendations
for resolving the remaining issues.
